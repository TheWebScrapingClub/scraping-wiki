# The importance of scraping inventory levels data in the retail industry

> Summary of an article published on [The Web Scraping Club](https://www.scraping.club/?utm_source=wiki&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=article_summary) on 2024-08-04 (free to read). **[Read the full article](https://www.scraping.club/p/scraping-inventory-levels?utm_source=wiki&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=article_summary)**

Inventory levels are one of the most requested data points in retail, mainly for revenue forecasting and competitive intelligence. But the value depends on how a company sells. Using Nike as the example, most revenue comes through wholesalers and physical stores, so stock exposed on the brand's own website covers only a small slice of the business. And unlike prices, inventory data is not needed to sell online, so what a site shows, and how granular it is, varies a lot.

I wrote this from the real cases I saw in my years scraping fashion websites. We start from the revenue split of a brand and work down to the logistics behind the site. Stone Island is the worked example: we scraped the same item across countries to see which warehouses serve which markets, then compared the result with store-level availability. I also cover the granularity levels you meet across websites and the places where I usually look for stock data.

## What's inside

- How to read a brand's revenue split by channel to judge how much of the business a website's stock data can represent.
- Why summing inventory country by country can count the same items several times when warehouses serve multiple countries.
- The levels of granularity sites expose, from no data to exact numbers, and which use case each one fits.
- A caveat on the add-to-cart check: why it should not be part of your scraper.

**In the full article:** The Stone Island per-country inventory results, the screenshots of the store availability data, the field names to look for in page HTML and the full list of places to find stock data.
